## VERSI PANJANG — ISI POST (English)

I joined ASTRNT seven years ago as a front-end developer. I remember the version of me who sat down on day one: excited, eager to prove myself, and sure that the most important thing was shipping good code fast.

ASTRNT taught me otherwise. Our products were used by real people, a candidate preparing for an assessment, a recruiter choosing who to bring in, and code mattered less than whether those people actually got what they needed. So I grew, from building screens to owning features from the first conversation to the final result. Behind that growth was one person who kept asking questions I did not always want to answer.

I still remember one of my first lessons with Nigel Hembrow, because it stung. I found a flow in the frontend where an API request ran with no loading state. Eager to help, I changed the flow on my own, quietly, around the SOP. Nigel called it out, and he was right. Any change to the product, even a small one, has to be known. The product manager has to see it, and it is not finished until it is understood, recorded and agreed. That day I learned to communicate before I act, and to respect the process even when I think I am helping.

My second lesson came during a progress report. I had finished what I was asked to build and said so with pride. Nigel did not ask how it worked. He asked who it was for, why they needed it, and how we would know the objective was met. I had no good answer, and that question followed me for years, until I stopped asking "can I build this?" and started asking "should we, and for whom?" He made me prove it too. Before building, he wanted validation from real users, and he was willing to be the user himself. When I worked on the recruiting product, he sat on the other side as a hiring manager, and I wrote his feedback into my notes like any customer's. He let data make the call instead of assumptions.

The daily syncs built the discipline. Camera on, out of respect for each other's time. The Jira ticket on the table. Who you need help from. Who is blocking you. Why you built what you built, told from the customer's side. Every ticket carried a due date, and he pushed us to move not in rigid sprints but in a steady rhythm of rapid development, because a growing company needs momentum. That discipline is with me today.

Then AI changed how we work, and Nigel showed me how to stay ahead of it. He taught me to treat AI as a partner, not a shortcut, one willing to poke holes in my thinking before I build on it. He showed me how to be T-shaped in the AI era: deep in one craft, broad enough to see the whole product. And he gave me a belief I now carry. The future is not about AI replacing us. It is about brilliant people becoming even more brilliant with the right tools.

To everyone at ASTRNT, the engineers, designers and product people who shared this ride: thank you for the collaboration, the honest feedback and the memories. Building alongside you made me better in ways I cannot count.

I walked in wanting to prove I could write code. I leave knowing why the code exists, who it serves, and how to tell when it truly works. Thank you, ASTRNT, for that. 🚀

See you in the next chapter!

### ISI POST (English)

I joined ASTRNT seven years ago as a front-end developer, convinced that the most important thing was shipping good code fast. I leave as a full-stack engineer who knows that code only matters when it serves real people: a candidate preparing for an assessment, a recruiter finding the right person for a role. The question that changed my career is the one I asked myself more and more: who is this for, and why does it matter to them?

Most of that change I owe to Nigel Hembrow, my CEO, my mentor and my coach. As a CEO he kept the bar high. He wanted value, not features. He asked for validation from real users before we built, he let data make the call instead of assumptions, and he ran us with discipline: camera-on syncs, every Jira ticket with a clear due date, and no silent changes to the product. Early on he called me out for making a change around the SOP. He was right, and I never forgot it.

As a mentor he stretched my thinking. He taught me to treat AI as a partner, not a shortcut, one willing to poke holes in your ideas before you build on them. He showed me how to be T-shaped in the AI era: deep in one craft, broad enough to see the whole product. And he gave me a belief I carry with me. The future is not about AI replacing us. It is about brilliant people becoming even more brilliant with the right tools.

To everyone at ASTRNT who shared this ride, thank you. Building alongside you made me better. I walked in wanting to prove I could write code, and I leave knowing why the code exists and who it serves. Thank you, ASTRNT. 🚀

See you in the next chapter!
